Fernando Botero

Fernando Botero Angulo

Fernando Botero Angulo was a Colombian painter and sculptor, widely regarded as the most recognized Latin American artist of his time. His signature "Boterismo" style depicts people and figures in large, exaggerated volume, blending political criticism with humor and satire.

Biography

Fernando Botero Angulo (April 19, 1932 — September 15, 2023) was a Colombian painter and sculptor whose distinctive style, known as "Boterismo," made him one of the most celebrated Latin American artists in history. His work is characterized by the exaggerated, voluminous portrayal of people and figures, a technique he used to explore themes of power, politics, satire, and everyday life.

Early Life and Education

Botero was born in Medellín, Colombia, in 1932. His father, a traveling salesman, died when Botero was just four years old. He attended a Jesuit school and, inspired by the Baroque architecture and religious art he encountered, began drawing and painting at an early age. In 1944, his work was first exhibited in a group show in Medellín. He later studied at the Liceo de la Universidad de Antioquia and worked as an illustrator for the newspaper El Colombiano.

In 1948, at age 16, Botero had his first solo exhibition in Medellín. He moved to Bogotá in 1951, where he held his first solo show at the Galería Leo Matiz. His early work was influenced by Francisco de Goya, Diego Velázquez, and the Mexican muralists.

European Influences

In 1952, Botero traveled to Europe with a group of artists. He spent time in Madrid, studying the works of the Old Masters at the Museo del Prado, and later in Paris and Florence. In Florence, he studied the Italian Renaissance painters, particularly Paolo Uccello, Piero della Francesca, and Giotto. His time in Europe profoundly shaped his artistic vision, leading him away from abstraction and toward a figurative style with a distinct volumetric emphasis.

The Emergence of "Boterismo"

By the late 1950s, Botero began developing the style that would become his signature. His figures became increasingly plump and round, not as a critique of weight or body shape, but as an aesthetic choice to emphasize volume, sensuality, and monumentality. He famously said, "An artist is attracted to certain kinds of form without knowing why. You adopt a position intuitively; only later do you attempt to rationalize or even justify it."

His 1961 painting Mona Lisa at Age Twelve, which reimagined Leonardo da Vinci's masterpiece with a youthful, rotund figure, marked a turning point in his career and drew international attention.

Career and International Recognition

Throughout the 1960s and 1970s, Botero's work gained international acclaim. He moved between New York, Paris, and Bogotá, and his paintings and sculptures were exhibited worldwide. His art can be found in prominent public spaces, including Park Avenue in New York City, the Champs-Élysées in Paris, and the streets of Madrid, Buenos Aires, and his native Medellín.

In addition to paintings, Botero created numerous bronze sculptures, many of which are monumental in scale. His sculptures retain the same exaggerated proportions as his paintings, depicting animals, figures, and everyday scenes with a sense of playfulness and dignity.

Later Work and Philanthropy

Botero continued to paint and sculpt well into his later years. He generously donated many works to museums in Colombia, particularly the Museo Botero in Bogotá and the Plaza Botero in Medellín, which houses 23 of his monumental sculptures. In 2012, he was awarded the U.S. International Center for the Recognition of Artistic Merit.

In his final years, Botero focused on a series of paintings depicting the violence in Colombia, including works inspired by the conflict between drug cartels and the government. He also created a series on Abu Ghraib, publicly denouncing the abuses depicted. These works were donated to museums around the world.

Death and Legacy

Fernando Botero died on September 15, 2023, at his home in Monaco at the age of 91. He left behind a monumental legacy as one of the most important Latin American artists in history. His "Boterismo" style remains instantly recognizable, and his works continue to be exhibited and celebrated in museums and public spaces across the globe.

His impact on Colombian and Latin American culture is immeasurable. Through his generosity and philanthropy, he ensured that future generations would have access to world-class art. The Museo Botero in Bogotá, which houses his donation of 208 works (123 of his own and 85 by other artists), stands as a testament to his enduring legacy.
